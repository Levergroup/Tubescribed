To **repurpose video content for email**, don't just send a "new video is up" link. Pull the transcript, split the video into its main ideas, and turn each idea into one short email that teaches something on its own. A 10 to 20 minute tutorial with three clear sections can become a three-email sequence: one email per idea, sent a day or two apart, each ending with a link to the full video.

This guide gives you a step-by-step workflow, a mapping table you can copy, and a real 11-minute YouTube video we ran through TubeScribed, including what we had to fix before it was ready to send.

## Why a sequence beats a "new video" email

Most creators treat email as a megaphone for their videos. The email says "watch my new video," a few people click, and the rest of the list learns nothing.

When you repurpose video content for email properly, the email does the teaching. The video becomes the optional deep dive. That changes three things:

- **Readers get value without clicking.** Many people read email on their phone in short bursts. A written idea they can use right away beats a link they save for later.
- **One video lasts longer.** Instead of one announcement, you get several emails from the same recording.
- **The emails can run on autopilot.** A sequence, which GetResponse defines as a series of automated emails sent on a set timetable ([GetResponse on email sequences](https://www.getresponse.com/blog/email-sequence)), keeps working for every new subscriber, long after the video is published.

If you want a single newsletter issue instead of a sequence, our guide to [video to newsletter AI](/blog/video-to-newsletter-ai) covers that format. This article focuses on sequences.

## How to repurpose video content for email in 6 steps

### Step 1: Pick a video with clear parts

The best source videos have structure: numbered steps, a framework, a before-and-after story, or a list of mistakes. Each part becomes one email. A rambling vlog or a short video with one idea is better as a single email.

Only repurpose videos you own or have permission to use.

### Step 2: Get the transcript

You need the words in text form. YouTube shows a free transcript under many videos with captions. For a clean full text you can edit, a transcription tool is faster, especially for videos without captions.

### Step 3: Map the video into emails

Before you write anything, list the video's main ideas and give each one a job in the sequence. Use the table in the next section. This is the step most people skip, and it's why their emails read like a chopped-up transcript.

### Step 4: Write each email around one idea

Each email needs:

1. **A subject line** that names the idea or the problem it solves.
2. **An opening line** that connects to the reader's situation.
3. **The idea itself**, written for reading, not speaking. Cut filler words, repetition and on-camera asides.
4. **One action** the reader can take today.
5. **A link to the video** for readers who want the full walkthrough.
6. **A teaser** for the next email, so the sequence feels connected.

### Step 5: Add the video the right way

Don't try to embed a playable video. [Mailchimp's help center](https://mailchimp.com/help/add-video-to-an-email) explains that video can't play directly in an email program. Instead, it uses a thumbnail image that looks like a player and opens the video in the browser. Do the same in any email tool: a linked thumbnail or a plain text link.

### Step 6: Load it into your email tool and set the timing

Add the emails to an automation in your email platform and space them out. Daily spacing suits a short sequence that builds on itself; the welcome series in our example below sends each email the day after the last. Make sure every email has a visible unsubscribe link. [Google's email sender guidelines](https://support.google.com/mail/answer/81126?hl=en) require one-click unsubscribe for senders of more than 5,000 messages a day to Gmail, and a clear way out is good practice at any size.

## The video-to-email map you can copy

Here's a simple way to repurpose video content for email when the video is a tutorial or framework. Fill in one row per idea.

| Video section | Email role | Send | What the email does |
|---|---|---|---|
| Intro and the main problem | Email 1: Welcome or hook | Day 0 | Names the problem, gives one quick win, links the video |
| First main idea or step | Email 2: Teach | Day 1 or 2 | Explains one idea in writing with an example |
| Second main idea or story | Email 3: Proof or story | Day 3 or 4 | Shares a story or example and suggests a next step |
| Summary or call to action | Final line of Email 3 | Same | Says what happens next and how to work with you |

Two rules keep the map useful: one idea per email, and every email should make sense if it's the only one someone reads.

## Real example: we ran an 11-minute video through TubeScribed

To test this on a real video, we picked a public tutorial that is itself about email sequences: [Welcome Email Sequence Tutorial: 3 Emails Every Creator Should Send](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=tInGzWgfZXY) by Cody Burch on his YouTube channel. It runs 11 minutes 28 seconds. He walks through a three-part welcome series: a first email that welcomes the subscriber and points to your offer, a second that tells your own before-and-after story, and a third built around a client success story.

We pasted the link into TubeScribed. Audio recognition didn't succeed on this video, so TubeScribed fell back to the captions and labelled the transcript "from captions" (2,860 words). That used 2 credits, because the video starts a second 10-minute block. We then generated an **Email Newsletter** from the transcript, which used 1 more credit, so 3 in total. The output was a 402-word email with five subject line options, an intro, a section on each of the three emails, a sign-off and a P.S.

Here's a short excerpt of the real output:

> **Subject line options**
> - **Number:** My 3-part "Silver Platter" welcome sequence
> - **Question:** Are you hiding the cash register?
>
> **1. The Buffet Cart (Email #1)**
> Don't just deliver a PDF and disappear. Make them feel welcome (tell them you did a backflip when they joined). Tell them they aren't alone anymore. Most importantly, give them the **"Easy Button."** If they have more money than time, show them exactly how to skip the line and work with you immediately. Never hide the cash register.

That's a faithful summary. In the video, he says it plainly: "don't hide the cash register."

**What we had to fix:**

- **It was one email, not three.** The Email Newsletter output summarizes the whole video in a single message. For a sequence, we split it at the three numbered sections and expanded each into its own email, using the map above.
- **Invented labels.** The output named the second email "The Vulnerability Bridge" and the third "The Pedestal." The speaker uses "buffet cart" for the first email, but the other two names aren't his. We'd drop them or mark them as our own.
- **A detail that contradicts the video.** The output mentions "the problem that created five other problems." In the video, he suggests going only two levels deep on the problem a problem creates. We changed it to match.
- **Added benefits.** The example benefits "more time with family, more freedom, less stress" don't come from the video. We'd replace them with the reader's real outcomes.
- **Caption errors.** Because the transcript came from captions, a few words were off, such as "email score" where the video description names his program Email School. Check names against the video before you quote anything.

Our verdict: the structure, subject line ideas and the first section were usable as a starting draft and saved the slowest part, which is pulling the ideas out of 11 minutes of talking. It took one careful edit and a split into three emails before it was ready to load into an email tool.

## Which videos make good email sequences

Not every video deserves a sequence. Use this quick decision table.

| Video type | Best email format | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Step-by-step tutorial | 3 to 5 email sequence | Each step is a natural email |
| Framework or method | 3 email sequence | One email per part of the framework |
| Webinar or workshop | Sequence plus a recap email | Long videos hold several ideas; see our guide to [converting a webinar to a blog post](/blog/convert-webinar-to-blog-post) for the written version |
| Coaching call or Q&A | Several single emails | Each good question becomes a standalone email; our guide to [repurposing coaching call content](/blog/repurpose-coaching-call-content) shows how |
| Short tip or one idea | One email or newsletter section | Not enough material to split |
| News or update video | Newsletter mention | Dated content doesn't suit an evergreen sequence |

## Mistakes to avoid

- **Pasting the transcript.** Spoken language reads badly. Rewrite for the eye: short paragraphs, one idea, clear headings.
- **Making every email a link.** If the email only says "watch this," it isn't repurposed content. Teach first, link second.
- **Trying to embed the video.** Use a linked thumbnail or text link, as Mailchimp recommends.
- **Copying claims without checking.** Speakers round numbers, mention prices that change, and make claims that need a source. Check anything specific before you send it to your list.
- **Forgetting the next step.** The last email in a sequence should tell readers what happens next, whether that's your regular newsletter, a call or an offer.
