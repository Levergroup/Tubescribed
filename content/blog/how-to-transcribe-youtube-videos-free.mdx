Here is **how to transcribe YouTube videos free**: open the video on a computer, expand the description, and click "Show transcript". YouTube's own help page says you can see the full transcript of any video that has captions. If the video has no captions, or you need clean text you can edit and reuse, you need a second method. This guide covers three, shows where each one breaks, and ends with a real hour-long video we ran through TubeScribed.

## How to transcribe YouTube videos free with the built-in transcript

This is the fastest free route, and it needs no account or software.

1. Open the video on a computer.
2. Expand the description below the video.
3. Click **Show transcript**. The transcript opens next to the video and scrolls as the video plays, according to [YouTube Help](https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/15930243?hl=en).
4. Click any line to jump to that moment in the video.

[Zapier's guide](https://zapier.com/blog/youtube-video-transcript) also covers the mobile app steps, and notes that the option to hide timestamps is on desktop only. If you want clean copy-paste text, use a computer and switch timestamps off first.

**Where it breaks:**

- **It only exists when the video has captions.** No captions, no transcript panel. [Riverside's guide](https://riverside.com/blog/youtube-transcription) lists common reasons: the creator turned captions off, the video is music only, the language isn't supported, or a live stream hasn't ended yet.
- **There's no download button.** The guides we read all describe the same workaround: select the text, copy it and paste it into a document.
- **The text is rough.** Pasted transcripts usually have no punctuation and no speaker labels, so you have to tidy them before they're readable.

If you only need to find a quote or skim a short video, Method 1 is enough. If you plan to reuse the text, keep reading.

## Method 2: Download your own captions in YouTube Studio

If the video is **yours**, YouTube Studio is a better source than the viewer panel. Riverside's guide explains that creators can download their own caption files from Studio, in formats such as VTT, SRT and SBV. These are timed caption files, so they work for subtitles, but you'll still need to strip the timing codes if you want plain text.

Two practical notes:

- **Edit before you export.** YouTube's automatic captions are a draft. Fix names, product terms and punctuation in Studio first, so the file you download is the corrected one.
- **You can only do this for channels you manage.** For someone else's video, Method 1 or Method 3 is your route.

## Real example: we ran a 63-minute video through TubeScribed

To test Method 3 on a real, public video, we used [Captions, Transcripts, and Audio Description: What Your Video Actually Needs](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=TqXv0BErl8c), a live stream by Natalie McLeese of Aardvark on video accessibility. It runs 62 minutes 50 seconds, which is longer than the 5 to 30 minutes we normally pick, but it is a public video about captions and transcripts, so it suits this topic.

We pasted the link into TubeScribed. It transcribed the audio, not the captions, and returned 9,659 words. That used 7 credits (62:50 rounds up to seven 10-minute blocks). We then generated a **Key Takeaways** output from a trimmed excerpt of the transcript covering captions, transcripts and caption quality, which used 1 more credit, so 8 in total. The output came back as a 447-word list of eight takeaways with a short summary.

Here is a short excerpt of the real output:

> **Implement Speaker Identification and Sound Cues:** Captions must go beyond words. Explicitly label different speakers (e.g., "Narrator:" or "Terrell:") and describe significant non-speech sounds (e.g., "[Suspenseful music builds]")...
>
> **Humanize "Auto-Generated" Captions Through Manual Editing:** Never publish raw AI-generated captions. Manually correct common phonetic errors (e.g., changing "surfaces" to "services" or "roll" to "enroll")...

**What we had to check and fix:**

- **Framing that isn't the speaker's.** The output pushes a "non-negotiable baseline" and a "mentality" to reject. In the video, the speaker argues captions need to be very accurate, but she makes that case as a rough illustration, not a measured statistic. If you reuse it, present it as her view, not a fact.
- **A made-up example.** The "[Suspenseful music builds]" caption above is the output's own. In the video, the speaker only complains that her sample captions said "music" with no detail.
- **A dropped item.** The speaker lists the quality keys for captions and the output names five, leaving out "equal", meaning captions that match what is said, including profanity.
- **Added claims.** Words like "abandonment" and "Searchable Intelligence" are not from the speaker, and the chart example in the audio description point isn't hers either. She uses a mask and a water bottle.
- **Style.** A heading with an emoji and punchy lines don't fit every brand voice.

Our verdict: the transcript itself was the useful part. The video even shows why: the speaker plays a clip where YouTube-style auto-captions turn "services" into "surfaces", and the audio transcript didn't repeat that kind of error in the passages we read. The takeaways were a good first pass at structure and needed one careful read against the video before we'd publish them. For a one-hour talk, that is a lot less work than listening to it twice.

## When TubeScribed is not the right tool

Be honest about fit. Skip TubeScribed if:

- **You need a quick look at a short, captioned video.** The built-in panel is free and instant.
- **The video isn't a public YouTube video.** TubeScribed works from public YouTube URLs only, not private videos, uploaded files or other sites.
- **The video is longer than 90 minutes.** That is the limit, so split the recording first.
- **You need legal-grade or certified captions.** For compliance work, an edited caption file reviewed by a person is the safer path, as the accessibility talk above argues.
- **You only want subtitles for your own video.** YouTube Studio already does that.

## How to turn a free transcript into something useful

Once you have the text, the work is editing and reuse:

1. **Fix names and terms.** Search for proper nouns and product names, since those are what automatic transcription gets wrong.
2. **Add paragraphs and speaker labels** if more than one person talks.
3. **Check every quote against the video** before you publish it.
4. **Pick a format.** A transcript can become a blog post, a FAQ, social posts or an email. Our guide to [turning a YouTube transcript into a blog post](/blog/youtube-transcript-to-blog-post) walks through it, and our [complete guide to repurposing YouTube content](/blog/repurpose-youtube-content) shows the bigger picture.

Only reuse videos you own or have permission to use. Quote briefly, credit the speaker and link the source.
